# Logistics & Supply Chain Management

## From Understanding to Practice

## 1. Home — Behind Every Product

### Every product has a journey. Every journey needs a plan.

The backpack you carry, the food you buy, and the medicine a pharmacy receives all depend on people and systems working together.

Materials must be purchased. Products must be made. Stock must be stored. Orders must be prepared. Deliveries must reach the right place. Information must stay accurate along the way.

Logistics and supply chain management help these activities work together, so customers receive what they need and businesses use their resources carefully.

This guide makes that world easier to understand. You can explore how it works, what people do, which skills matter, and how to begin learning without industry experience.

## 2. Understand the Field

### What is logistics?

Logistics is the planning and management of the movement and storage of goods, together with the information needed to support that movement.

It includes receiving products, storing them, preparing orders, arranging transport, and handling returns.

For our backpack store, logistics includes keeping backpacks in suitable storage, finding the right item when an order arrives, packing it, and arranging delivery.

### What is supply chain management?

Supply chain management looks at the wider network that makes a product available to a customer. It connects demand, suppliers, purchasing, production, inventory, logistics, and the organisations involved.

It asks how these activities can work together to meet customer needs while managing cost, resources, and uncertainty.

For our store, this includes deciding which backpacks to sell, selecting suppliers, planning stock, coordinating deliveries, and responding when something changes.

### How do they relate?

Logistics is an important part of supply chain management. Logistics focuses on movement and storage. Supply chain management also considers the wider relationships and decisions that make those activities possible.

| Area | A simple question |
| --- | --- |
| Demand planning | What might customers need? |
| Procurement | What should we buy, and from whom? |
| Production | How will the product be made? |
| Inventory management | How much stock should we hold? |
| Logistics | How will goods be stored and moved? |
| Supply chain management | How will all these activities work together? |

### Who is involved?

Suppliers provide materials or products. Manufacturers turn materials into finished goods. Warehouses store and prepare goods. Transport providers move shipments. Distributors and retailers help products reach markets. Customers create demand and receive the final service.

One organisation may perform several of these roles. Some products follow short journeys; others move through many businesses and locations.

### Three flows to understand

**Goods:** Materials, products, and returns move between locations.

**Information:** Orders, forecasts, stock records, delivery updates, and other details guide the work.

**Money:** Payments, costs, refunds, and credit arrangements support the commercial relationships.

These flows do not always move in the same direction or at the same time. A return may move back towards the seller, while a refund moves towards the customer.

### Common misunderstandings

Supply chain work includes much more than delivery. It involves planning, data, purchasing, systems, and coordination.

Holding more stock does not automatically solve every problem. Stock can improve availability, but it also uses space and money.

Technology helps when processes and information are reliable. Software alone cannot fix unclear responsibilities or inaccurate records.

## 3. The Complete Supply Chain Journey

### Follow one backpack, from an idea to a delivery

The journey below is simplified. Real supply chains work as networks, with activities happening at the same time and information moving between them.

### Step 1 — Understand demand

The store considers which backpacks customers may want, in which colours, and in what quantities. Past sales, seasonal patterns, and planned promotions can support this estimate.

The main question is: **What are customers likely to need?**

If demand is underestimated, products may sell out. If it is overestimated, the store may hold stock that moves slowly.

### Step 2 — Plan supply

The team compares expected demand with available stock, incoming deliveries, storage space, and budget. It decides what to order and when.

The main question is: **How can we prepare for demand with the resources we have?**

Planning continues as sales and supply conditions change.

### Step 3 — Source and purchase

The store evaluates suppliers using factors such as product quality, price, reliability, lead time, and order conditions. It confirms quantities and delivery expectations.

The main question is: **Which supplier can meet our needs reliably?**

The lowest purchase price may come with longer delays or additional costs.

### Step 4 — Produce and prepare

The manufacturer obtains materials, schedules work, makes the backpacks, checks quality, and prepares them for shipment.

The main question is: **Can the product be made to the required quality and timing?**

Material shortages or production problems can affect every later stage.

### Step 5 — Receive and store

When the shipment arrives, the warehouse checks quantities and condition. Accepted goods are recorded and placed in suitable storage locations.

The main question is: **Do the physical goods match our records?**

Missing or incorrect records can cause problems when customer orders arrive.

### Step 6 — Receive the customer order

A customer chooses a backpack. The store checks availability and passes the order information to fulfilment.

The main question is: **Can we fulfil the order we have accepted?**

The order needs accurate product, quantity, address, and service information.

### Step 7 — Pick, check, and pack

The correct backpack is collected from storage, checked against the order, and packed for delivery. The shipment receives the necessary label and documentation.

The main question is: **Are we sending the correct item in suitable packaging?**

An error here can lead to a return, a replacement, and a disappointed customer.

### Step 8 — Transport and deliver

The shipment is handed to a carrier and moves through its delivery network. Tracking updates help the store and customer understand its progress.

The main question is: **Will the order arrive as promised?**

Delays, damage, or an incomplete address may require action.

### Step 9 — Support and returns

The store responds to delivery questions and any return request. Returned goods are inspected and routed according to their condition and applicable policies.

The main question is: **How can we resolve the issue and handle the returned product responsibly?**

### Step 10 — Learn and improve

The team reviews errors, delays, costs, and feedback. It looks for repeated problems and updates the process.

The main question is: **What should we change before the next order?**

## 4. Inside the Work

### What do people actually do?

Much of the work involves making plans, checking information, coordinating people, and responding when reality differs from the plan.

### Demand and supply planning

Planners review demand, stock, incoming supply, and capacity. They update forecasts and identify possible shortages or excess stock.

**A small example:** Blue backpacks sell faster than expected. The planner checks whether an earlier replenishment is possible.

### Procurement and supplier coordination

Procurement teams clarify purchasing needs, compare suppliers, manage purchase orders, and follow up on delivery commitments.

**A small example:** A supplier changes a delivery date. The coordinator checks the effect on stock and agrees on the next action.

### Inventory management

Inventory teams monitor quantities, investigate differences, arrange stock counts, and support replenishment decisions.

**A small example:** The system shows twenty backpacks, but the shelf contains eighteen. The team investigates before accepting more orders.

### Warehouse operations

Warehouse teams receive goods, organise storage, pick orders, pack shipments, and prepare dispatch. Supervisors plan work and check that procedures are followed.

**A small example:** Frequently ordered backpacks are placed where they can be reached easily, while safe access and storage rules are maintained.

### Transport coordination

Transport teams arrange collections, compare service options, track shipments, and manage delivery exceptions.

**A small example:** A carrier misses a collection. The coordinator checks alternatives and updates the affected team.

### Customer and internal communication

Clear updates help people act on the same information. A useful update explains what happened, what it affects, who is taking action, and when the next update is expected.

### Process improvement

Improvement work starts by understanding a problem. Teams collect evidence, investigate causes, compare solutions, and check whether the change helps.

**A small example:** Similar product labels cause picking mistakes. The team improves identification and reviews the error rate afterwards.

## 5. Foundations Before You Start

### Begin with understanding. Build experience through practice.

You can start learning without industry experience. A useful foundation includes basic business knowledge, confidence with numbers, careful handling of information, and an interest in how processes work.

You do not need to master every topic at once. These foundations give you a starting point for further learning and role-specific preparation.

### Business basics

Understand customers, demand, sales, cost, profit, and service. Learn how poor availability, errors, and delays can affect a business.

**You understand this when you can:** Explain why a low delivery price may still lead to a costly outcome if orders are frequently damaged.

### Supply chain structure

Learn the main actors and how they depend on one another. Understand that a supplier's problem can become a warehouse or customer problem later.

**You understand this when you can:** Draw a simple supply chain for a product you use.

### Basic maths

Practise percentages, averages, quantities, unit conversions, time calculations, and simple costs. Always check units and assumptions.

**You understand this when you can:** Calculate that a delivery cost of €120 across forty orders averages €3 per order, while recognising that individual orders may cost different amounts.

### Data literacy

Learn to read tables, recognise missing values, identify duplicates, and ask what a number represents. A useful analysis starts with trustworthy information.

**You understand this when you can:** Find orders delivered after their promised date and explain how you identified them.

### Spreadsheet foundations

Learn sorting, filtering, basic formulas, dates, and simple charts. Practise using clearly labelled columns and consistent units.

**You understand this when you can:** Maintain a small stock table showing opening quantity, receipts, issues, and closing quantity.

### Process thinking

Understand inputs, activities, outputs, responsibilities, and dependencies. Notice where work waits and where mistakes occur.

**You understand this when you can:** Map an order from receipt to dispatch and explain who handles each step.

### Inventory foundations

Learn about stock levels, demand, lead time, replenishment, stockouts, and safety stock. Understand that stock decisions involve both availability and cost.

**You understand this when you can:** Explain why uncertain demand or delivery timing can make replenishment harder.

### Warehouse and transport foundations

Understand receiving, storage, picking, packing, dispatch, shipment, and delivery. Learn why product condition, weight, size, and handling needs matter.

**You understand this when you can:** Describe how a fragile product may need different handling from a durable backpack.

### Information and communication

Learn why accurate product codes, addresses, quantities, and status updates matter. Practise asking clear questions when information is incomplete.

**You understand this when you can:** Write a short shipment update that separates confirmed facts from information still being checked.

### Your starting-point check

Can you explain a product journey, read a simple stock table, calculate a basic cost, map a process, and communicate a problem clearly?

If some answers are still difficult, that gives you a useful learning plan. Choose one area and practise it before moving on.

## 6. Skills You Need to Build

### Knowing a concept and using it are different steps

Knowing what lead time means is knowledge. Using lead time to prepare a replenishment plan is a skill. Showing your assumptions and calculations makes that skill easier to assess.

### Start here

**Clear communication:** Explain needs, problems, and next actions in language others can understand.

**Attention to detail:** Check product codes, quantities, dates, and addresses. Small errors can create larger problems later.

**Basic analysis:** Compare numbers, notice patterns, and ask questions before drawing conclusions.

**Organisation:** Keep track of tasks, deadlines, records, and priorities.

**Teamwork:** Understand how your work supports other people and share information when it matters.

### Build next

**Problem-solving:** Describe the problem, collect evidence, investigate causes, and test a suitable response.

**Planning:** Match demand with stock, time, capacity, and available resources.

**Systems thinking:** Consider how a change affects the wider process.

**Decision-making:** Compare alternatives using clear criteria and explain your reasoning.

**Project coordination:** Define tasks, responsibilities, dependencies, and completion checks.

### Develop for your chosen role

Depending on your direction, you may build deeper skills in forecasting, purchasing, negotiation, inventory planning, transport, warehouse design, analytics, or system implementation.

Your role, industry, and working environment will shape which skills need the most attention.

## 7. Tools, Technology & Information

### Understand the purpose before learning the software

Tools help people record information, coordinate activities, make decisions, and control work. Begin by asking what problem a tool is meant to solve.

| Tool or technology | What it helps with | Backpack example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spreadsheets | Small calculations, records, and analysis | Reviewing stock and weekly sales |
| ERP: Enterprise Resource Planning | Connecting business records and processes | Linking purchases, inventory, sales, and finance |
| WMS: Warehouse Management System | Managing warehouse activities and locations | Guiding receipt, storage, picking, and dispatch |
| TMS: Transportation Management System | Planning and managing transport | Organising shipments and reviewing transport performance |
| Barcodes and RFID | Identifying items and capturing data | Recording a product or shipment movement |
| Dashboards | Making selected performance information visible | Showing delays and order accuracy |
| Material handling equipment | Moving, storing, or sorting goods physically | Moving stock within a warehouse |

### Information quality matters

A useful system depends on accurate product data, reliable transactions, clear responsibilities, and people who understand the process.

If a backpack is placed in the wrong location or a receipt is not recorded, a digital system may present an inaccurate picture of availability.

### Automation and analytics

Automation can support repeated tasks, such as transferring order information or generating an alert. Analytics can help people understand patterns and compare options.

Start by defining the task, required information, and exceptions. Then decide where technology can help and where a person needs to review the result.

### A beginner practice

Create a simple spreadsheet for ten products. Give each product a code, description, stock quantity, and location. Record a receipt, a sale, and a return. Check whether the final record matches the movements you entered.

## 8. Decisions & Systems Thinking

### A good decision considers the whole journey

Supply chain decisions often involve trade-offs. Improving one result may make another result harder to achieve.

### Cost and speed

A faster delivery service may cost more. A slower service may be suitable when the customer accepts the timing. Compare the service requirement with the total cost and likely consequences.

### Inventory and availability

More stock can reduce some shortage risks, but it also uses money and space. Too little stock may cause missed sales or urgent replenishment.

The aim is to choose stock levels that fit demand, supply uncertainty, and business priorities.

### Efficiency and resilience

A tightly planned system may use resources efficiently but have little room for disruption. Alternatives, spare capacity, or additional stock may improve resilience, while adding cost.

The right balance depends on the consequences of a failure and the resources available.

### Local improvements and overall performance

Picking orders faster may create a queue at packing. Buying larger quantities may reduce purchase prices but increase storage pressure.

Ask: **Where does the work go next, and what happens there?**

### Bottlenecks

A bottleneck is a part of the process that limits its overall capacity. Improving another step may have little effect if that constraint remains.

Look at how much work each stage can handle, where queues form, and why.

### Measure what matters

| Measure | What it helps you understand |
| --- | --- |
| Delivery reliability | Whether deliveries meet the agreed timing |
| Order accuracy | Whether customers receive the correct items and quantities |
| Inventory accuracy | Whether recorded stock matches physical stock |
| Lead time | How long a defined activity or journey takes |
| Cost per order | The selected costs associated with handling an order |
| Capacity utilisation | How much of the defined available capacity is being used |
| Customer feedback | How customers experience the service |

Define each measure carefully. State the time period, included costs or activities, and data source. Different definitions can produce different results.

## 9. Design, Implement & Improve

### Turn a business need into a working process

A logistics system brings together people, processes, information, equipment, and physical space. Designing it begins with understanding what it needs to achieve.

### 1. Understand the need

Describe the products, customers, order volumes, service expectations, and current problems. Separate confirmed facts from estimates.

### 2. Define requirements

Identify what the system must handle. Consider storage, product protection, order information, capacity, safe working conditions, budget, and delivery expectations.

### 3. Map the process

Show how goods and information will move. Define who owns each step, what they receive, and what they pass on.

### 4. Compare options

Consider different layouts, service providers, equipment, or system choices. Compare them using requirements and clear criteria.

### 5. Plan implementation

Break the work into tasks. Agree responsibilities, dependencies, training needs, and completion checks. Consider how existing work will continue during the change.

### 6. Test the process

Try ordinary cases and exceptions. Can the team handle a normal order, an incorrect receipt, an unavailable item, and a return?

Check both the physical activity and the information recorded.

### 7. Commission the system

Commissioning means checking that the system is ready for its intended operation. Confirm that people, equipment, records, and procedures work together as required.

### 8. Review and improve

Monitor performance after launch. Investigate problems, adjust the process, and check the effect of each change.

## 10. Industry Scope & Working Environments

### One field, many settings

The same basic ideas appear across different industries, but the products and service needs change the work.

| Setting | Examples of important considerations |
| --- | --- |
| Manufacturing | Material availability, production schedules, and supplier coordination |
| Retail and e-commerce | Product availability, order fulfilment, delivery, and returns |
| Food | Shelf life, suitable storage, product condition, and traceability |
| Healthcare | Product integrity, availability, traceability, and applicable controls |
| Logistics services | Coordinating storage or transport for several customers |
| Spare parts and maintenance | Keeping necessary parts available when equipment needs support |

### Different places to work

Work may take place in an office, warehouse, distribution centre, factory, transport operation, or a combination of environments.

Some roles focus on analysis and planning. Others involve daily physical operations, supplier relationships, system projects, or team supervision. Working schedules and demands vary by role and employer.

### Local, European, and global networks

International supply chains add distance, partners, currencies, documentation, and different operating conditions. Depending on the route and goods, customs requirements may also apply.

European operations can involve road, rail, air, and sea transport, with decisions about service, cost, capacity, and environmental impact.

Detailed legal or customs decisions require current information for the specific goods and route.

### Responsible operations

Supply chain decisions can affect waste, packaging, energy use, transport impacts, and working conditions. Responsible improvement considers these effects alongside cost and service.

## 11. Careers & Roles

### Explore the work behind the title

Job titles vary between organisations. Read responsibilities carefully and look at the knowledge, tools, experience, and working conditions each role requires.

### Procurement and sourcing

**Purpose:** Help the organisation obtain suitable goods or services from suppliers.

**Typical work:** Preparing purchase orders, comparing offers, maintaining supplier records, and following up on commitments.

**Useful preparation:** Business basics, communication, spreadsheet skills, cost comparison, and attention to detail.

**Example titles:** Procurement Assistant, Purchasing Coordinator, Buyer.

**Possible development:** More responsibility for suppliers, categories, negotiation, and purchasing strategy.

### Demand and supply planning

**Purpose:** Help match expected demand with available supply and resources.

**Typical work:** Reviewing sales, preparing forecasts, checking stock, and updating replenishment plans.

**Useful preparation:** Data analysis, basic statistics, inventory concepts, and careful handling of assumptions.

**Example titles:** Planning Assistant, Demand Planner, Supply Planner.

**Possible development:** Responsibility for wider planning processes, product ranges, or planning teams.

### Inventory and warehouse operations

**Purpose:** Keep goods controlled, accessible, and ready for fulfilment.

**Typical work:** Receiving, recording, counting, storing, picking, packing, and investigating stock differences.

**Useful preparation:** Product identification, process discipline, stock records, and relevant workplace training.

**Example titles:** Warehouse Operative, Inventory Assistant, Inventory Controller, Warehouse Coordinator.

**Possible development:** Supervision, warehouse planning, improvement, or operational management.

### Transport and logistics coordination

**Purpose:** Help goods move according to the agreed service.

**Typical work:** Booking shipments, coordinating carriers, checking documents, tracking deliveries, and resolving exceptions.

**Useful preparation:** Organisation, communication, transport basics, and accurate record keeping.

**Example titles:** Logistics Assistant, Transport Coordinator, Shipping Coordinator.

**Possible development:** Responsibility for larger transport operations, provider relationships, or network planning.

### Supply chain analysis

**Purpose:** Use information to understand performance and support decisions.

**Typical work:** Cleaning data, building reports, identifying patterns, and comparing scenarios.

**Useful preparation:** Spreadsheets, quantitative reasoning, data interpretation, and clear explanation.

**Example titles:** Supply Chain Analyst, Logistics Analyst, Operations Analyst.

**Possible development:** Deeper analytics, improvement projects, or decision-support responsibilities.

### Systems and improvement

**Purpose:** Help processes and business systems work better together.

**Typical work:** Mapping processes, gathering requirements, testing changes, preparing documentation, and supporting users.

**Useful preparation:** Process thinking, digital confidence, problem-solving, and project coordination.

**Example titles:** Logistics Systems Analyst, Process Improvement Analyst, Implementation Coordinator.

**Possible development:** Larger implementations, specialist design work, or project leadership.

### Operations and management

**Purpose:** Coordinate people, resources, priorities, and performance.

**Typical work:** Planning capacity, supervising teams, handling exceptions, reviewing results, and improving operations.

**Useful preparation:** Operational understanding, leadership, judgement, and relevant experience.

**Example titles:** Operations Supervisor, Warehouse Manager, Logistics Manager, Supply Chain Manager.

Management roles commonly involve responsibility developed through experience. Career progression can move between functions as well as towards leadership.

## 12. Start Without Experience

### Build understanding, then show what you can do

A small, well-explained exercise can demonstrate how you think. Keep your assumptions visible and describe learning projects honestly.

### Stage 1 — Understand the journey

Choose a familiar product. Identify suppliers, production, storage, delivery, and returns.

**Create:** A simple supply chain map.

**Check:** Can another person understand the journey from your explanation?

### Stage 2 — Work with numbers and records

Create sample products, stock quantities, receipts, and orders. Practise formulas, sorting, and checking balances.

**Create:** A basic stock spreadsheet.

**Check:** Do the quantities reconcile after each movement?

### Stage 3 — Map an operation

Describe how an order is received, checked, picked, packed, and dispatched.

**Create:** A process map with responsibilities.

**Check:** Is every step clear, including what happens when an item is unavailable?

### Stage 4 — Compare decisions

Compare two suppliers or delivery services using sample information. Consider cost, timing, reliability assumptions, and customer needs.

**Create:** A short recommendation with reasons.

**Check:** Have you explained the trade-offs and uncertainty?

### Stage 5 — Build a small project

Combine your work into a fulfilment plan for a fictional online store.

**Create:** A compact project showing the problem, process, records, decisions, and exception handling.

**Check:** Can someone follow your reasoning and identify the limits of the model?

### Stage 6 — Choose your next direction

Reflect on which tasks interested you most. Planning, operations, purchasing, analytics, and systems work offer different learning directions.

**Create:** A short reflection covering strengths, gaps, and the next skill to practise.

This path supports learning and preparation. Employers may also require particular qualifications, experience, languages, or training for individual roles.

## 13. Practice Lab — A Small Online Store

### Plan a simple backpack fulfilment system

**Project type:** Simulated learning exercise.

**Scenario:** A fictional online store sells backpacks and needs a clear process for receiving stock, preparing orders, and managing returns.

### Learning assumptions

The store offers three backpack models. It receives an average of twenty orders per working day, with one backpack per order. A supplier usually takes ten working days to replenish stock. The store uses one small storage area and an external delivery provider.

These figures are invented for practice. They are not business results or forecasts for a real company.

### The problem

Stock records are inconsistent, storage locations are unclear, and staff do not have a shared process for handling unavailable items. The store wants to understand and organise its operation before selecting more advanced tools.

### What the process needs

Each product needs a clear code and storage location. Receipts and dispatches need to be recorded. Orders need accurate item and address information. Goods need suitable handling and packaging. Exceptions need an owner and a next action.

### Proposed process

1. Receive stock and compare it with the expected shipment.
2. Check condition, record accepted quantities, and assign locations.
3. Receive an order and confirm the available quantity.
4. Pick the product using its code and location.
5. Check the item against the order, then pack and label it.
6. Hand the shipment to the carrier and record dispatch.
7. Monitor exceptions and update the customer when needed.
8. Inspect returns and update the product and financial records through the appropriate process.

### A simple stock calculation

At the assumed average of twenty orders per working day, a ten-working-day replenishment period corresponds to 200 backpacks of expected demand across the range.

This is a learning calculation, not a complete reorder recommendation. A practical plan would also consider demand by model, variability, available and incoming stock, outstanding orders, and supplier uncertainty.

### A delivery decision

Two fictional delivery options are available:

| Option | Price per shipment | Stated delivery window |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Standard | €4 | 3–5 working days |
| Express | €7 | 1–2 working days |

Express costs €3 more per shipment. If twenty orders all use Express, the additional quoted transport cost is €60 for that day's orders.

Standard may be suitable when its timing meets the customer promise. Express may suit customers who need a shorter window. These quotes do not prove actual reliability, and other costs or conditions would need checking.

### Test a disruption

Suppose the supplier reports an additional five-working-day delay. At the same average demand, that represents another 100 backpacks of expected demand across the range.

Check stock by model, open orders, and incoming shipments before deciding what to do. Possible responses include agreeing a partial shipment, checking an alternative source, or revising availability promises.

### Review performance

Track whether stock records match physical stock, whether orders contain the right products, whether dispatch meets the plan, and why returns occur.

Set clear definitions before measuring. Keep the original data so the results can be checked.

### Reflect on the limits

This exercise does not establish safe storage capacity, staffing needs, legal compliance, or a complete inventory policy. Those decisions need more information and, where appropriate, specialist input.

The purpose is to practise connecting a business need with a process, records, decisions, and checks.

### More exercises to explore

**Supplier comparison:** Compare price, timing, quality information, and reliability assumptions.

**Stock shortage investigation:** Trace the difference between recorded and physical stock.

**Warehouse layout:** Plan locations that support access, product protection, and a clear flow of work.

**Returns improvement:** Identify repeated return reasons and suggest a change that can be evaluated.

## 17. Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I start learning without experience?

Yes. Start with the product journey, basic numbers, simple records, and process thinking. Small exercises can help you build understanding before you gain workplace experience.

### Do I need advanced maths at the beginning?

Begin with percentages, averages, units, time, and simple costs. Some specialist roles need deeper statistics or optimisation, which you can study as your direction becomes clearer.

### Do I need to learn every software system?

Start by understanding what systems do and by becoming comfortable with spreadsheets. More specific software knowledge depends on the role and organisation.

### Is logistics mainly warehouse and delivery work?

Warehousing and delivery are important parts. The wider field also includes planning, purchasing, inventory, information systems, analysis, and improvement.

### What should my first project be?

Choose a familiar product, map its journey, create a stock table, and explain how one order will be fulfilled. Include one problem, such as a late supplier delivery, and describe your response.

### How do I know which career direction fits me?

Try different tasks. Notice whether you prefer analysing information, coordinating people, organising physical work, comparing suppliers, or improving systems. Use that experience to guide further learning.
